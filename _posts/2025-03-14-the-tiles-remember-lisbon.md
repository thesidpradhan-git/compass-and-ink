The first tile I noticed wasn't in a museum or a church — it was on the side of a laundromat on Rua de São Bento, half-covered by a election poster from two cycles ago, glazed blue and white and cracked down the middle like a wishbone. I stood there long enough that a woman came out to ask if I was lost. I wasn't lost. I was just doing the thing you do in Lisbon before you know you're doing it, which is looking up, and sideways, and down an alley you hadn't planned on, because the city keeps putting something in front of you that wasn't in the guidebook.

Lisbon is a city that wears its history on the outside. Other cities bury it — a plaque here, a museum wing there, history filed away so the present can get on with itself. Lisbon just tiles over it, quite literally, and lets you read the sediment. A seventeenth-century pattern gives way to an Art Nouveau one gives way to a 1980s replacement panel in the wrong blue, and nobody's in a hurry to fix the mismatch. You start to understand that the mismatch *is* the fix.

## The elevator that goes nowhere useful

Everyone tells you to ride the Elevador de Santa Justa, and everyone is a little bit wrong to, because the view from the top is fine and the queue is an hour and there is a footbridge two minutes away that gets you the same view for free. I rode it anyway, on my third day, mostly because my friend Inês insisted that the point was never the view.

"You're not going up to see something," she said, wedged next to me in the cast iron cabin, an elderly man's shopping bag pressed against both our knees. "You're going up to remember that this used to be how people got anywhere. Before it was a thing tourists do, it was just — Tuesday."

That's the sentence that's stayed with me longer than anything in the miradouros. Half of what we call a tourist attraction is just somebody else's Tuesday, dressed up and given a queue. I don't think that makes it worthless. I think it means the job, if you're passing through, is to find the seam where the Tuesday is still showing.

> A city doesn't perform its past for you. You just have to be quiet enough, long enough, to catch it not performing.

## Where the seam shows

I found it, eventually, not in Alfama — too photographed, too aware of itself now — but in Graça, on a Sunday morning when the flea market was still setting up and half the stalls were just a blanket and a box of somebody's grandmother's things. An old woman was selling a stack of azulejos pried, she said without embarrassment, from a building being demolished near Anjos. Twelve euros a tile. I bought two — one cracked, one whole — and she wrapped them in newspaper from three days prior, so that unwrapping them at home in Kyoto's opposite, my kitchen, months later, I got a headline about a transit strike I'd forgotten I'd walked straight into.

That's the souvenir I'd recommend, if you want one thing to carry home: not a fado CD, not a tin of sardines with an ironic label, but something that was already there before the city became a destination, sold to you by someone who remembers it as ordinary. It's a small, cracked argument against the idea that a place exists for your benefit. Lisbon was getting on fine, tile by tile, before any of us showed up with a camera — and it'll keep layering long after the last tram tour has gone home.

I think about that laundromat wall sometimes. Whether the poster's been taken down, whether the crack's spread further, whether anyone else stood in front of it the way I did, annoying some woman trying to get to her washing. I hope somebody did. That's the whole trick of the city, as far as I can tell — it rewards the people willing to be a small nuisance to themselves, standing still in a place built for moving through.
