By the time I reached the second rest stop, I'd stopped counting gates. The guidebooks all cite the same number — ten thousand, give or take — as if that settles anything. It doesn't. Ten thousand is a statistic. What you actually experience is closer to a tunnel that keeps deciding not to end, vermillion on both sides, close enough to touch, close enough that the light comes through orange even at noon.

## The Gate That Isn't a Gate

Every torii on the mountain was paid for by someone — a business, a family, a person who wanted a wish taken seriously by whichever god was listening. Their names run down the back of each post in black kanji, which means you're not really walking through a shrine. You're walking through several centuries of other people's hope, stacked one gate deep, all the way up Mount Inari.

I didn't know that going in. I found out from a shopkeeper near the second torii cluster, selling little fox-faced charms to anyone who paused long enough to make eye contact.

> "People think they're praying to the gate," she said. "They're not. The gate is just what gratitude looks like when you make it permanent."

### The Foxes Are Watching, Technically

Inari is the god of rice, commerce, and — depending which shrine literature you trust — prosperity in general, and the fox is Inari's messenger, not Inari itself, a distinction almost every visitor gets wrong on purpose because the fox statues are so much better company. Stone foxes line the paths in pairs, keys or jewels held in their mouths, and by hour two of the climb I'd started nodding to them like colleagues.

### What the Climb Actually Costs

Nobody warns you clearly enough that the famous photograph — the tunnel of gates, empty, glowing — requires either arriving before six in the morning or climbing well past the point most tour groups turn back. The mountain has an honest way of sorting its visitors:

- The first twenty minutes: everyone. Tour buses, school groups, the full crowd from the postcards.
- Past Yotsutsuji intersection, halfway up: a third of that crowd, maybe less.
- The summit, Ichinomine: whoever actually wanted to be there.

Three things I'd tell anyone climbing it for real:

1. Go at dusk, not dawn — the crowds thin the same amount, and you get the gates half-lit instead of half-asleep.
2. Bring cash for the tea houses partway up; card machines don't survive the humidity.
3. Don't rush the descent. The gates behind you look nothing like the gates in front of you.

---

I didn't make it to true sunset — the last cable car down the parallel ropeway stops early, and I'd underestimated how long ten thousand gates take when you keep stopping to read the names. But I got far enough up that the tourist noise fell away entirely, far enough that it was just foxes, gates, and the specific quiet of a mountain that's been thanked by strangers for a very long time. If you want the version of Kyoto that isn't waiting for rain to earn its quiet, read [Kyoto in the Wrong Season](/essays/on-slowness/) — this is the same city, arguing with itself in a different key.
