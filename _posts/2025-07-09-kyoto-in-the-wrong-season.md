I timed it wrong, or so everyone kept telling me before I left. Not cherry blossom season, not the maple-leaf weeks of November that turn the eastern hills into something a postcard company invented. I arrived in the first week of July, deep in tsuyu, the rainy season, when the humidity sits on the city like a hand and the forecast is the same soft gray promise every single morning: rain, then more rain, then a break around four that fools you into leaving your umbrella at the ryokan.

I want to make the case for arriving wrong.

## The gardener at Saihō-ji

Saihō-ji — the moss temple, famously requiring a written request weeks in advance just to be allowed through the gate — turns out to want the rain more than it wants the sun. I learned this from a gardener named Mr. Watanabe, who I cornered, gently, after the sutra-copying ritual that precedes entry, and who spoke enough English and enough patience to actually answer a foreigner's overeager questions.

"Tourists ask when the moss is most beautiful," he said, working a stray leaf out from between two cushions of green with something between tweezers and chopsticks. "I tell them: now. Always now. But they want a season. Moss doesn't have a season, it has weather." He gestured at the rain, which had picked back up, drumming softly on the maple canopy above the garden's hundred and twenty varieties of moss, each one a slightly different register of green, glowing with the specific saturation that only comes from being properly, thoroughly wet.

He was not being poetic for my benefit. He'd clearly said some version of this sentence to a hundred visitors before me, in the mildly weary tone of a man who has made peace with being right about something people don't want to hear. Cherry blossoms are a two-week argument for coming to Kyoto. Moss, he seemed to be telling me, is a permanent one — you just have to be willing to get wet to see the point being made.

> The city everyone photographs and the city that's actually there are not always the same city. Sometimes the second one only shows up when the weather chases the first one indoors.

## What the rain empties out

The practical upside of arriving in the wrong season, nobody warns you about in advance: the crowds thin to almost nothing. I stood alone — genuinely alone, no jostling for a sightline — at the Philosopher's Path, at Nanzen-ji's aqueduct, at a dozen small shrines that in April would have required patience and a wide-angle lens to photograph without fifty strangers in frame. Rain does something cherry blossom season can't: it edits. It removes exactly the people who came for the postcard, and leaves behind the ones who, for one reason or another, couldn't wait for better weather — which turns out to be a much more interesting group to share a temple garden with.

I don't want to overcorrect into telling you to avoid the famous weeks — I've seen photos of Kyoto under cherry blossoms and I understand exactly why people plan a year around it. But I'd gently push back on the idea that there's a *right* time to see a place, as opposed to a marketed one. Every season is doing something to a city; the popular ones just happen to photograph well. Kyoto in July was humid, damp-socked, quietly frustrating in the way that carrying an umbrella for six straight days is frustrating — and it was also the only time I've stood in a five-hundred-year-old moss garden with nobody's voice in it but a gardener's, telling me, correctly, that I'd arrived exactly on time.
