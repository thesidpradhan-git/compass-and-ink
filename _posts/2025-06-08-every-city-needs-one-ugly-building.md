There's a specific kind of city center — usually European, usually photographed relentlessly, usually the entire reason for the trip — that after three days starts to feel like walking through a beautifully maintained museum with no exhibits. Every façade coordinated. Every balcony the correct shade of shutter-green. Nothing ugly, and therefore, somehow, nothing quite real.

The cities I've actually wanted to stay in all have at least one genuinely ugly building — a brutalist car park, a 1970s office block that everyone agrees is a mistake, a shopping center nobody photographs on purpose. And I've come to think that ugly building is doing more honest work than the postcard streets around it. It's proof the city kept building through decades it wasn't trying to look good for. It's evidence of an actual, ongoing argument about what the place should become, rather than a settled agreement to preserve what it already was.

Prettiness, past a certain point, is a kind of stopping. The ugly building is what a city looks like when it's still deciding.
