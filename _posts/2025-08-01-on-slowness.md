An itinerary looks like the least ideological document a person can produce. It's a spreadsheet, or a shared doc, or a folded paper map with times scrawled in the margins — pure logistics, surely, no worldview required. I want to argue the opposite: an itinerary is one of the more concentrated pieces of ideology most of us write in a given year, precisely because it's disguised as neutral planning. Every itinerary is a set of claims about what a place owes you, how much of it can be known, and how fast a human being can reasonably be expected to understand somewhere they've never been. Those are not logistical questions. They're beliefs, wearing a schedule's clothes.

## The belief hiding in "must-see"

Start with the phrase that anchors most itineraries: *must-see*. It sounds like a simple ranking — this is better than that, prioritize accordingly — but sit with it a moment longer and it reveals a much stranger claim: that a handful of locations can represent a place well enough that seeing them constitutes having *seen it*, in some sense that would satisfy an outside observer asking whether your trip was worthwhile.

This is, when you say it plainly, a fairly aggressive piece of ideology. It assumes a place can be compressed. It assumes the compression is roughly the same for every visitor, which is why the same six sites appear on every list regardless of who's traveling or why. And it quietly assumes that the traveler's job is to *collect* rather than to *understand* — that arriving somewhere and photographing the must-sees discharges an obligation, checks a box, completes a transaction with the place itself. None of that is a neutral description of geography. It's a theory of what travel is for, and it happens to be a theory that serves the tourism economy extremely well, since compressible, collectible destinations are much easier to sell than open-ended ones.

> A must-see list isn't a shortcut to understanding a place. It's an agreement, mostly unexamined, about how little understanding a visit is allowed to require.

## Slowness as the counter-argument, not the aesthetic

"Slow travel" has become enough of a marketing term by now that I'm wary of using it — it's been absorbed into the same industry it was meant to critique, sold back as a boutique inconvenience for people who can afford a longer trip. I want to separate the aesthetic of slowness, which is mostly about vibes and long train rides and artfully unhurried breakfasts, from the actual argument underneath it, which is much less photogenic: that understanding a place takes time roughly proportional to how complicated the place actually is, and no itinerary, however cleverly routed, can negotiate a discount on that.

This isn't a claim that fast trips are worthless — a single well-spent day somewhere is real, and dismissing it as shallow is its own kind of ideology, the inverted snobbery of people who've mistaken duration for depth. The actual claim is narrower: that speed is a trade-off, not a hack. Every itinerary that promises you a comprehensive experience of somewhere in seventy-two hours is smuggling in the belief that comprehension scales with logistics, that if you just route the taxis efficiently enough you can outrun the actual limiting factor, which is that places are made of relationships, histories, and habits that don't compress no matter how good your driver is.

## What to do with a belief you can't fully escape

I'm not arguing for the abolition of itineraries — I plan trips with spreadsheets like everyone else, and I don't think that's shameful. What I've started doing instead is treating the itinerary the way I'd treat any other ideological document: as something to interrogate rather than simply execute. Before a trip now, I try to ask what belief a given must-see item is smuggling in — usually some version of *this is what makes the place legible to people who've never been* — and whether I actually agree with that belief, or I've just inherited it from twelve other people's blog posts.

Sometimes the answer is: yes, I agree, that cathedral really is worth the two hours. Sometimes the answer is: no, actually, and I'd rather spend those two hours in the market three blocks away where nothing is must-see and everything is, in the only sense that's ever mattered, actually there. The itinerary doesn't disappear either way. It just stops being something I follow, and starts being something I'm allowed to argue with — which, it turns out, is roughly the same shift this whole publication keeps arguing you should make with every other unexamined belief you happen to be carrying when you travel.
