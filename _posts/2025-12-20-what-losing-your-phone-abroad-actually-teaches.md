It fell out of a jacket pocket on a bus, and I didn't notice until I reached for it to check the time and my hand found nothing. Nineteen hours until a replacement SIM situation got sorted, in a city where I knew no one and spoke maybe forty words of the language.

The first hour was pure panic — no maps, no translation, no way to tell anyone where I was. The second hour was something closer to withdrawal, an actual physical restlessness in my hand from not having anything to hold. By hour four, something had quietly shifted. I was reading street signs instead of following a blue dot. I was asking people questions instead of asking a search bar. I got lost twice and found my way back both times using landmarks I'd had to actually notice, because noticing was the only tool I had left.

I'm not going to pretend I want to lose my phone again. But I got it back and felt, for about a week afterward, a strange grief for those nineteen hours — for how much more of the city I'd taken in with nothing to mediate it. I've since started leaving it in the hotel room for an hour most mornings. It's not the same. It's the closest I can get on purpose.
