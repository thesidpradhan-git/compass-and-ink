Day nine of a five-week trip, sitting in a guesthouse courtyard with nothing scheduled and no particular desire to go find something, it occurred to me that I was bored. Not tired, not homesick — bored, in the exact flat way I used to get bored on a rainy Sunday at home. This felt like a category error. I had, by any reasonable accounting, everything to be curious about and nothing to complain of.

Nobody writes about this part. Every account of long travel I'd read jumped from arrival excitement to some later epiphany, skipping the unglamorous middle where a person, even in an interesting place, occasionally just wants to lie on a bed and stare at a ceiling fan for an hour, and does.

I've decided the boredom isn't a failure of the trip. It's just what happens when a place stops being new and hasn't yet become familiar enough to be comfortable — a genuine, unphotogenic stage that long travel simply requires you to pass through, the same way a long friendship requires the unremarkable Tuesdays as much as the good nights out. I let myself be bored that afternoon. The next morning, for no reason I can name, the city was interesting again.
